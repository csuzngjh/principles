# Series 9 | AI Remembers What We Did, but Not Always Why

**Subtitle**: From "A Goal Is Not a Prompt" to the Long-Term Memory of Agents

## 01 After the Watch Changes, Who Still Remembers the Heading?

In [the previous article, *A Goal Is Not a Prompt*](/abyss/08-goal-not-a-prompt), we told the story of the USS Santa Fe.

Captain Marquet wanted his crew to stop obeying orders mechanically, and instead understand the intent behind each mission — and volunteer their own judgment.

From "Tell me what to do" to "I intend to...", it looks like nothing more than a change of phrasing. Beneath it lies a shift from obeying instructions to taking part in decisions.

But the story can be pushed one step further.

A submarine may sail for months, and the crew rotates through positions every day.

Suppose that at every watch change, the crew member coming on duty sees only the previous shift's operation logs, without knowing why this route was chosen, why certain waters were avoided, or why an alternative plan was abandoned.

Then, even if everyone has mastered the operating procedures, they may still fail to make the right call in a new situation.

A ship's log can record where the ship has been.

**But a ship's log alone cannot tell those who come after why this path was taken.**

![A traveler sits on a cliff looking back at the road behind him: a golden path of light strings together memories of bookings, handshakes, meetings, and storefronts, winding toward a distant summit planted with a flag.](/images/abyss/09/abyss-09-memory-path.webp)

This brings me to today's AI Agents.

We can already have them complete complex tasks, and we have begun letting them remember past conversations, knowledge, and experience.

Yet one question remains:

**Does remembering what we did mean that an AI understands why we did it?**

## 02 Why Does AI Remember So Much, Yet Still Not Truly Understand Us?

Imagine a scenario.

A founder is building a product that helps barbershops improve their customer experience.

He tells the AI:

"We want to help our stores earn customers' trust."

From then on, the AI accompanies him through a great deal of work.

On day one, they optimize the booking feature.

On day two, a customer reports: although she had booked a time slot, she still waited more than half an hour after arriving at the store.

On day three, the founder starts discussing how to attract new users, and the AI proposes a marketing campaign.

Every task gets done well.

The AI may remember the technical implementation of the booking system, keep the customer's feedback, and record the marketing plan.

![A founder sits before his screens, where a set of holographic barbershop memories floats: customers waiting in line, a barber serving a client, a storefront marked with a butterfly sign, and a growth chart — together forming a roadmap toward a distant summit.](/images/abyss/09/abyss-09-barbershop-plan.webp)

But here is the problem.

When the founder is about to invest more resources in attracting new users, will the AI proactively remind him:

"We found earlier that customers who booked were still kept waiting a long time. Perhaps before drawing more people into the store, we should fix that first. Otherwise, the more new users arrive, the more bad experiences we create."

That sentence requires no astonishing reasoning ability.

What it truly requires is connecting things that happened at different times, and understanding why they matter.

The booking feature, the customer's complaint, and the marketing plan look like three separate tasks.

But they all point to the same longer-term question:

**Are we actually helping the store earn customers' trust?**

If the AI searches its memory only around the current task, the complaint about waiting half an hour remains an archived piece of feedback.

But if it keeps hold of the long-term product intent, this seemingly ordinary piece of feedback may become the basis for the next important decision.

The difference here is not whether the AI has stored that information.

It is whether the AI knows when it should be recalled.

## 03 Memory Is Not a Warehouse; It Is a Choice

I recently read an Anthropic research article on Agent Memory, and one line stayed with me:

*Memory is, occasionally, a prediction.*

Memory is, at times, a prediction.

When we decide to remember something, we are implicitly making a judgment: this experience might still be useful later.

![A human and an AI agent sit side by side at a porthole window, where a golden path of light strings together barbershop memories outside, stretching toward a lighthouse on the cliff — intent is like the lighthouse, guiding memory toward its direction.](/images/abyss/09/abyss-09-intent-lighthouse.webp)

This made me rethink long-term memory for Agents.

Today we focus heavily on how to make AI remember more.

How to store conversations, compress context, build knowledge associations, and make retrieval more accurate.

![A corridor of memory stretches through digital space, countless memory cards suspended amid currents of blue data, while a great golden ring-shaped device turns quietly in the distance.](/images/abyss/09/abyss-09-memory-corridor.webp)

These technologies certainly matter.

But there seems to be a more fundamental question:

**If the AI does not know where the future is headed, how can it judge which past experiences are worth remembering?**

Consider ourselves first.

Someone just learning to cook may remember the first time they burnt a dish.

An experienced chef, however, does not necessarily remember every failure of heat control. What he more easily remembers is how a particular ingredient behaves under unusual conditions.

Someone preparing to open a restaurant cares, beyond cooking technique, especially about customer reviews, ingredient costs, and operational risk.

The same experience can mean different things to different people.

That is because our memory is shaped not only by past events, but also by long-term interests, identity, responsibility, and expectations of the future.

Of course, humans also remember plenty of trivial things; memory is not a precise value calculation.

But at least one point is worth pondering:

**Truly valuable memory is not only about what happened in the past; it also includes what that past has to do with the future.**

Many of today's Agents lack exactly this kind of continuing connection.

Every session has its own task.

When the task is done, the session ends. The next time it starts, the AI may reload its memory, yet it does not necessarily carry a continuously evolving background of goals and judgment.

It has history, but not necessarily coherent experience.

It has knowledge, but does not necessarily know which knowledge matters most to the person in front of it.

So I began to consider a possibility:

Could an Agent's memory be organized not around isolated tasks, but around a relatively persistent intent?

For now, I call this research direction **Persistent Intent Anchored Adaptive Memory (PIAAM)**.

The name sounds technical, but the idea is simple:

**Let AI not only remember what we did, but decide, based on what we want to achieve over the long term, which experiences are worth carrying into the future.**

![A human and an AI agent gaze together at floating memory cards; on the ring-shaped base below, the words "long-term intent" glow, and a golden pillar of light anchors every memory to the same direction.](/images/abyss/09/abyss-09-intent-ring.webp)

## 04 Some Things Are Worth Remembering; Some Should Slowly Fade

Here is another interesting question.

If an AI's memory keeps growing, does it become smarter and smarter?

Not necessarily.

We have a similar experience ourselves.

When we learn to ride a bicycle as a child, we may fall many times.

But years later, we hardly remember where each fall happened or why we lost balance.

What truly remains is the gradually formed sense of balance.

Many concrete experiences have faded, yet that does not mean they were worthless.

They may have been absorbed into the way we do things now.

I hope something similar happens for AI.

For example, a coding Agent has lived through many failed software releases.

It does not need to permanently remember every error message, every log, every fix.

More valuable may be a gradually formed habit of judgment:

A successful release does not mean users can actually use it smoothly.

Therefore, before a critical release, it proactively considers the real environment, the operating process, and the risks that might have been missed.

Of course, this does not mean AI has internalized experience into skill the way humans do.

Today's systems mostly improve performance by storing, organizing, and retrieving external memory.

But it points to a direction worth exploring.

**The purpose of long-term memory may not be to keep the past forever visible, but to let the valuable parts of the past keep working when they are needed.**

![In a dark circular memory vault, countless memory scenes sleep in a sea of blue data, while one barbershop moment is lit by golden light — what gets recalled is what truly matters now.](/images/abyss/09/abyss-09-memory-light.webp)

Some experiences need to be preserved for the long term.

Some details can gradually fade out.

Some knowledge that once applied should temporarily lose its influence after the environment changes.

And when a similar situation reappears, they should have the chance to be awakened again.

Forgetting, then, is not necessarily a failure of memory.

Appropriate forgetting may be exactly what helps us focus on the present and prepare for the future.

## 05 But What If We Set Off in the Wrong Direction?

Having written this far, one question cannot be avoided.

If long-term intent decides which memories are worth keeping, what happens when that intent itself is wrong?

Take the founder from earlier.

At first he believed customers were not using the product because its features were not rich enough.

So the AI kept accumulating experience around "adding features," remembering development tricks and product proposals.

But after months of real feedback, they gradually discovered:

What customers truly needed was perhaps not more features, but a simpler and more reliable service experience.

If the AI remembers only the experiences that support the original direction, it may keep helping the founder move faster down the wrong road.

So memory must preserve another kind of value:

**It should not only help us hold on to the directions worth holding on to; it should also help us discover when it is time to change direction.**

This is the double-loop learning discussed in the previous article.

Beyond correcting errors in action, we sometimes need to re-examine the assumptions behind the action.

For future AI Agents, I look forward to the day when, having accumulated enough evidence, it can say to its human:

"We used to believe the problem was here, but recent experience suggests we may need to rethink."

It should not change humanity's long-term goals on its own.

But it should be able to use past experience to help humans see the facts they may have overlooked.

Thus the relationship between goals and memory is no longer one-way.

Goals shape what we remember.

And memory may also change how we understand our goals.

It is a mutually shaping, continuously evolving process.

![A human and an AI agent sit side by side on a mountaintop, looking back over a golden path of light strung with scenes of meetings and storefronts — the path leads to a summit planted with a flag, and reminds them to re-examine the direction at any time.](/images/abyss/09/abyss-09-recheck-peak.webp)

## 06 From Remembering the Past to Carrying It Forward

Back to the Santa Fe.

A submarine can complete a long voyage not only because of precise operational records.

It also needs crew who understand the mission, know the environment, accumulate experience, and make fresh judgments as conditions change.

The long-term memory of AI Agents should perhaps develop in the same direction.

We certainly need better storage, more accurate retrieval, and more effective knowledge organization.

But these alone are not enough.

If we hope AI becomes a collaborator that can accompany humans through long-term work, learning, and growth, we may also need it to understand:

Why we made certain choices in the past.

Which experiences changed our judgment.

Which goals are still worth pursuing.

And which things we once firmly believed have been proven by reality to deserve re-examination.

![A human and an AI agent gaze into the distance together: a path of memory light crosses seas of clouds and mountains, stringing together scenes of meetings, collaboration, and storefronts, reaching toward the dawn on the horizon.](/images/abyss/09/abyss-09-co-journey.webp)

This remains an idea worth exploring, not a technical problem already solved.

But it makes me more and more convinced:

**Good memory is not about never forgetting. It is about knowing what is worth remembering, what can be let go, and when to recall it.**

In the previous article, we asked:

When agents learn to execute, who is responsible for judgment?

Now I want to push the question one step further:

If judgment depends on past experience, then across task after task, how should AI carry forward the meaning behind that experience?

After all, someone who truly understands us is not the person who can repeat every sentence we ever said.

It is someone who, even after a very long time, still understands what we care about — and can remind us when it matters:

**Do you still remember why we set out in the first place?**
